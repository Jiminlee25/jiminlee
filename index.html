Automated Skittles Color Sorter

A portable device that sorts mixed Skittles into five removable color bins after the user pours them into a funnel.

The problem

Our clients asked for a portable, affordable, automatic device that accurately sorts and stores different colored Skittles, as a stand-in for an industrial sorting task. They ranked accuracy above everything else.

Key design decisions

  • Vertical carousel to feed one candy at a time. We chose it over other concepts because it balanced single-candy throughput with mechanical simplicity.
  • Adafruit TCS34725 RGB sensor, which has its own white LED, so readings stay consistent regardless of room lighting.
  • NEMA 17 stepper to index the carousel in exact 90° steps, and an HS-311 servo to aim the slide across a 180° range.
  • 16×2 LCD that shows the count sorted per color, and raised bumps (1 to 5) on each bin so users with low vision can tell the bins apart.

My role Mechanical design lead

I owned the mechanical side of the project, from the first model to the assembled machine.

  • CAD: modeled the entire device in SolidWorks, including every custom part and the full assembly.
  • Part design: designed the funnel, carousel, slide, top and bottom frames, container base, and bins, plus the engineering drawings for each.
  • 3D printing: printed all of the PLA parts and reprinted them when fit or dimensions were off.
  • Integration: built the full structure and assembled all of the mechanical and electronic parts into the working machine.

Teammates led the circuit design, the Arduino code, and the system-level analysis.

How it works

Step 1Load

Candy poured into the 3D-printed funnel drops into one of the carousel's four pockets.

Step 2Scan

The stepper rotates the carousel 90°, placing the candy in front of the RGB sensor.

Step 3Aim

The Arduino classifies the color and turns the servo slide to that bin's angle.

Step 4Drop

Another 90° turn releases the candy down the slide while the next one is scanned. The machine stops after 8 empty rotations.

Design process

We defined objectives with the clients, then ranked them with a pairwise comparison chart. Color detection and sorting accuracy came out on top, followed by cost. We used a morph chart to compare ways to accept candy, detect color, sort, store, and power the device, and a glass-box analysis to map the functions between input and output.

Design evolution Why we rebuilt it

Our first detailed design used three servo motors. One pushed each candy out, and two more moved sets of blocks at the same time to steer it into the right color column. Once we started building, it ran into three problems:

  • Too many motors. Three servos meant more wiring, more power draw, more parts to keep in sync, and more places for the machine to fail.
  • Too slow. Every candy had to wait for several servos to finish moving before the next one could drop.
  • Wrong candy dimensions. After finishing the sketch we found our Skittle measurements were off, so the channels in the housing would not have worked with real candy.

We replaced the whole mechanism with a vertical carousel that feeds one candy at a time and a single rotating slide that aims it at the right bin. That cut the drive system to one stepper and one servo, let scanning and dropping happen in the same rotation, and gave a simpler structure to model and print. I redesigned the parts around the new layout with a ±1.5 mm tolerance.

Final part design

The final build combines 3D-printed PLA parts (funnel, carousel, slide, frames, container base) with laser-cut MDF back plates and acrylic bins. Several printed parts went through multiple revisions after dimensions came out wrong in the first prints.

Electronics and control

Prototype testing Mid-project checkpoint

Before the final build, we tested the sensor and the slide separately on a prototype.

  • Sensor test: held Skittles against the sensor for 1 second each. The reading matched the true color in all 4 recorded trials.
  • Rotation test: held each color to the sensor and checked whether the slide turned to the right bin. Red, orange, yellow, and green passed 4 of 4 trials. Purple failed all 4.

Purple became the focus of troubleshooting going into the final build. In final testing it sorted correctly 17 of 20 times.

What I'd change next

The power budget shows the stepper and its driver use most of the 67.88 W total, which explains the short battery life. Next time I would swap the stepper for a continuous-rotation servo, stiffen the frame, and enclose the wiring so it survives the drop test.

The project also taught our team to plan for reprints and failed parts, and to troubleshoot quickly when components shorted or stopped responding.

Unmanned Aerial Survey of Piermont Marsh

Why it matters

Piermont Marsh protects the Hudson's western shore from storm surge and filters contaminants. Rising seas, erosion, and invasive Phragmites australis are changing it quickly. Existing aerial data was low resolution, and past ground surveys were incomplete because the soft surface made them slow and difficult.

My role

  • Piloted the DJI Phantom 4 Pro on automated grid surveys planned in DroneDeploy, with the camera pointed straight down (90°).
  • Processed the image set in Agisoft Metashape Pro into the digital surface model.
  • Co-authored the research poster presenting the results.

Methods

Findings

  • Vegetation canopy is taller along creek banks near Tallman Mountain and gets shorter toward the river. Possible causes include more sediment and nutrient deposition near the mountain, or lower salinity there.
  • A transect through Tidal Creek shows a raised bank beside the channel that may be a levee.
  • Relative elevations are highly accurate. Absolute elevations are not, because the survey had no ground-truthed control points.
Future work

Install permanent ground control points to measure absolute elevation change over time, resurvey with LiDAR to separate bare ground from the canopy, and fly monthly surveys to study the marsh's annual growth cycle.
